How Europe Time keeps its clocks accurate

This page explains where the time data comes from, how offsets and DST are calculated, what the current EU policy status is, and what this tool deliberately does not do. It is maintained alongside the live clock at europetime.net.

1. Time zone rules: the IANA Time Zone Database

All offsets and DST transitions are calculated from the IANA Time Zone Database (tzdata), referenced by IANA zone names such as Europe/London, Europe/Paris, and Europe/Bucharest.

The calculation runs in your browser using the built-in Intl.DateTimeFormat API with a timeZone parameter. Browsers and operating systems ship with a copy of tzdata and update it periodically, so the page inherits those updates rather than relying on a hardcoded offset table.

The database is revised several times a year. Recent releases (2025–2026) included changes affecting European and global zones. If your browser or operating system is significantly out of date, a recent legislative change may not yet be reflected — keeping the device updated is the most reliable way to get current rules.

2. Clock source and precision

The displayed clock starts from your device's system time (JavaScript Date.now()). Device clocks are normally synchronized to UTC via NTP. Under typical network conditions, system clock accuracy is within tens of milliseconds of UTC — more than sufficient for scheduling purposes.

This is a browser-based clock, not an atomic-clock instrument. It does not claim sub-second traceability to a national metrology institute. If you need legally traceable time, use a certified time source.

The Time Travel slider adds or subtracts hours from the current instant and re-renders every zone; it does not change the underlying clock source.

3. DST policy status (as of November 2026)

  • Current law: Directive 2000/84/EC remains in force. Summer time begins on the last Sunday of March at 01:00 UTC and ends on the last Sunday of October at 01:00 UTC.
  • 2019: The European Parliament voted in favour of a proposal to end seasonal clock changes, conditional on Council agreement.
  • 2021: The proposal stalled in the Council; member states could not agree on whether to keep permanent summer or permanent winter time.
  • 2025–2026: The Commission signalled it might withdraw the proposal, but in March 2026 it still published transition dates through 2031. As of August 2026, the Council had reached no final decision.
  • Practical implication: seasonal changes continue for the foreseeable future. This page applies the rules currently encoded in tzdata; it does not predict future legislation.
  • Non-EU exceptions: Iceland (UTC+0 year-round), Russia (UTC+3 year-round), Belarus (UTC+3 year-round), Turkey (UTC+3 year-round).

4. Beyond the three zones

The homepage groups countries into three cards (WET/London, CET/Paris, EET/Bucharest). That grouping is a usability simplification, not a complete model of European time.

  • Iceland: Atlantic/Reykjavik, UTC+0 year-round, no DST.
  • UK, Ireland, Portugal: WET (UTC+0) in winter; UK and Ireland move to BST (UTC+1), Portugal to WEST (UTC+1) in summer.
  • Turkey: Europe/Istanbul, UTC+3 year-round since 2016, no seasonal change.
  • Russia: Europe/Moscow, UTC+3 year-round since 2014.
  • Belarus: Europe/Minsk, UTC+3 year-round.
  • Cyprus: the IANA zone is Asia/Nicosia (geographically in Asia), grouped on the homepage with the EET card. The zone name reflects IANA's geographic naming convention, not political geography.

5. What this page does not do

  • It does not provide historical time zone lookup for past dates.
  • It does not provide future-year DST projections beyond what the current tzdata release encodes.
  • It does not provide legally traceable time for contracts or regulatory purposes.
  • It does not cover every inhabited time zone; it focuses on Europe plus a set of commonly used comparison zones.

For reference-grade historical and future data, consult the IANA Time Zone Database directly or a dedicated time zone reference service.

6. Who maintains this tool

This tool is maintained by the team at China Quality Service, a third-party inspection company. Our daily work coordinating inspection windows with global importers — especially European buyers and factories — makes accurate local time a practical necessity. DST mismatches are not an abstract concern for us; they are a scheduling failure we have to avoid.

We are not a time-zone standards body. We are daily users of the IANA data, and we take responsibility for updating this page when the underlying rules or policies change.
